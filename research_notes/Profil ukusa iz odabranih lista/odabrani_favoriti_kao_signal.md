Method note: about 16 tool calls (web search plus fetch). Several primary PDFs could not be retrieved in full (Rentfrow & Gosling 2006, Cremonesi et al., Rashid et al. numeric tables). Where a claim comes from my background knowledge and was not checked in this session, it is marked **[UNVERIFIED]**.

## 1. Self-presentation and impression management in displayed taste (stated vs revealed preferences)

### Takeaway
Displayed favourites are a performance of taste. Liu (2007) showed this on 127k MySpace profiles, and Milkman et al. (2009) found people choose "should" items for the future but put off actually consuming them. So a curated list will lean towards prestige and identity compared with what people really consume. For matching people socially, though, that aspirational identity is probably the relevant signal: friendship selection on Facebook followed listed music and film tastes (Lewis et al. 2012), and shared music taste leads to attraction through inferred values (Boer et al. 2011).

### Cited Findings
- Liu (2007), "Social Network Profiles as Taste Performances", *J. Computer-Mediated Communication* 13(1):252–275, DOI 10.1111/j.1083-6101.2007.00395.x. It treats profile interest lists (music, books, films, TV) as a space for Goffman-style taste performance — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/13/1/252/4583067); [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/0d2a672c7610f079fbebc2ef2deba0a5)
- Liu analysed 127,477 MySpace profiles collected Nov 2006 – Jan 2007. The statistics support the existence of "prestige" and "differentiation" taste statements, meaning people list items to signal status and to set themselves apart — [synapsesocial abstract record](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2070119988)
- Later work keeps citing profiles as "taste performances" and examines how personae differ across multiple social networks (the same person shows different selves on different platforms) — [arXiv 1703.04791](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04791)
- Milkman, Rogers & Bazerman (2009), "Highbrow Films Gather Dust: Time-Inconsistent Preferences and Online DVD Rentals", *Management Science* 55(6):1047–1059. When choosing for the future rather than the present, customers find "should" films (highbrow, e.g. documentaries) relatively more attractive than "want" films (lowbrow, e.g. action). Customers keep should-DVDs significantly longer than want-DVDs before watching them, and the gap shrinks with experience. The data are 4 months of individual-level records from Quickflix, an Australian online DVD rental company, **not Netflix**. The common "Netflix queue" framing came from press coverage — [UPenn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/oid_papers/161); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:55:y:2009:i:6:p:1047-1059); [INFORMS press release](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/DVD-Rental-Companies-Forecast-How-Long-Customers-Will-Retain-Movies)
- Last.fm: users on average played tracks *less* after marking them "loved", partly because they love a track only after already listening to it a lot (Jawaheer et al., RecSys/HetRec 2010). A "favourite" mark is therefore a retrospective, identity-laden judgement, not a forecast of future consumption — [Jawaheer, Szomszor, Kostkova 2010 (UCL)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10088997/7/Jawaheer%2C%20Szomszor%2C%20Kostkova%20-%202010%20-%20Characterisation%20of%20explicit%20feedback%20in%20an%20online%20music%20recommendation%20service%281%29bh.pdf); [review summary](https://liner.com/review/characterisation-explicit-feedback-in-online-music-recommendation-service)
- Lewis, Gonzalez & Kaufman (2012), PNAS 109(1):68–72: across 4 years of Facebook data on one college cohort (~1,400 students per press coverage), students who shared listed tastes in **music and movies (not books)** were significantly more likely to become friends. Tastes mostly did *not* spread through friendships. The one exception was classical/jazz, which the authors read as a high-status cultural signal — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/01/taste-test/); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2012-01-facebook-patterns-music-movies-friends.pdf); [PNAS via zendy](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1073/pnas.1109739109)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Berger & Heath (2007, *J. Consumer Research*, "Where consumers diverge from others: identity signaling and product domains"): people diverge from majority choices mainly in *identity-relevant* domains (music, hairstyles, cars), and less in domains that signal little (e.g. bike lights). Music and films are therefore high-signal domains where displayed choices are shaped by identity.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Hogan (2010, *Bulletin of Science, Technology & Society*, "The presentation of self in the age of social media"): curated profiles are "exhibitions" put together for an imagined audience, not live performances. This fits deliberately curated favourites lists.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Lonsdale & North (2009/2011, *British Journal of Psychology*): people use music to manage identity and social impressions. Music is ranked as one of the most important leisure activities for identity, especially in adolescence.
- I found no peer-reviewed study that directly measures the gap between a user's *stated favourites list* and their *streaming history* (e.g. a Spotify "guilty pleasures" study). Industry anecdotes exist, but I did not verify them.

### Inferences
- Expect curated lists to over-represent prestige, distinctive and identity-defining items and to under-represent "want"/comfort consumption, especially when the list is public.
- For an events social network the bias may be a feature: Lewis et al. show friendship *selection* follows displayed taste, and Boer et al. (see section 6) show attraction runs through values that people infer from taste. Similarity in self-presented identity is plausibly the right target for "would you get along". It is the wrong target for "will you enjoy the same event playlist".
- Public vs private display probably changes the bias. A private "only for matching" mode may produce lists closer to actual behaviour (inference only, not tested here).

### Gaps
- No primary quantitative measure found of stated-favourites vs listening-history divergence for the same users.
- Effect sizes for Liu (2007) and Lewis et al. (2012) were not extracted; only abstracts and press were read.

## 2. Accuracy of personality and taste judgements from curated profiles (zero-acquaintance)

### Takeaway
Strangers judge personality with real but trait-specific accuracy from small curated taste displays: a top-10 song list, a bedroom, a social network profile. Openness is conveyed best. Conscientiousness and neuroticism are conveyed poorly. Profiles reflect actual rather than idealised personality (Back et al. 2010), so curation does not erase the signal.

### Cited Findings
- Rentfrow & Gosling (2006), "Message in a Ballad: The role of music preferences in interpersonal perception", *Psychological Science* 17(3):236–242. Study 1: music was the most common conversation topic among strangers asked to get acquainted. Study 2: observers formed consensual and accurate impressions of targets' personalities from targets' music preferences, and those preferences related to the targets' personalities — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507064/); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01691.x/)
- The stimulus was each target's list of **top 10 favourite songs**. Openness was communicated best, and conscientiousness was poorly conveyed — [PsyBlog summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/2007/02/personality-secrets-in-your-mp3-player.php). One secondary blog reports 74 student targets and 8 observers — [blog, secondary](https://lfno.blogspot.com/2006/04/songs-of-our-selves.html). **Sample sizes and correlations were not confirmed from the full paper.** **[UNVERIFIED]** From memory: observers also inferred values (e.g. openness-related values) and emotional stability above chance, and judgements were partly mediated by genre stereotypes (Rentfrow & Gosling 2007, "The content and validity of music-genre stereotypes", *Psychology of Music*).
- Rentfrow's dissertation at UT Austin: "observers were able to form consensual and accurate impressions about targets using nothing more than their favorite songs" — [TDL repository](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/handle/2152/2171)
- Back et al. (2010), "Facebook profiles reflect actual personality, not self-idealization", *Psychological Science* 21(3):372–374. N = 236 profiles from US Facebook and German StudiVZ/SchülerVZ. Observer ratings from profiles were related to targets' *actual* Big Five scores and *not* to their *ideal-self* ratings. Extraversion and openness were judged most accurately, and neuroticism was harder — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797609360756/); [Uni Mainz press](https://press.uni-mainz.de/?p=13247); [LJWorld](https://www2.ljworld.com/news/2010/mar/01/sites-seem-show-true-face/)
- Gosling, Ko, Mannarelli & Morris (2002), "A room with a cue", *JPSP* 82(3):379–398. 94 offices and 83 bedrooms. Independent observers agreed with each other (consensus) and showed some accuracy, generally stronger than in other zero-acquaintance research. Valid cues existed mainly for openness (distinctive decoration) and conscientiousness (neatness). Sex and race stereotypes partly mediated consensus and accuracy — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11902623/); [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2002/03/offices)

### Inferences
- A 10-item favourites list carries enough signal for strangers to infer Openness and value-related traits. It cannot say much about conscientiousness. A cross-domain taste profile should therefore present itself as being about *aesthetic/values identity*, not full personality.
- Back et al. suggest deliberate curation does not swamp real signal. Combined with section 1, a curated list is "real self, best foot forward" rather than fiction.
- Stereotype mediation means similarity scores built on genre labels will partly reproduce genre stereotypes. That is acceptable for matching, but it matters for explanations shown to users.

### Gaps
- Exact accuracy correlations by trait for Rentfrow & Gosling 2006 were not retrieved (full text not accessed).
- No study found on judging personality from favourite *films or books lists* specifically (as opposed to music or whole profiles).

## 3. How many items are needed for a stable profile?

### Takeaway
The recommender cold-start literature treats the number of elicited items as an accuracy vs effort trade-off. It shows that *which* items are elicited matters as much as how many. I found no verified plateau number in this session. Background knowledge suggests gains are steep up to roughly 10–20 ratings and flatten after that, but that is unverified. The reliability of favourites lists over time (test-retest) is essentially unstudied.

### Cited Findings
- Rashid et al. (2002), "Getting to Know You: Learning New User Preferences in Recommender Systems", IUI 2002 (DOI 10.1145/502716.502737). They compared item-selection strategies for new-user signup (random, popularity, entropy, popularity×log-entropy, personalised "item-item") offline on 7.5M MovieLens ratings and in a live study with 300+ new MovieLens users. The choice of strategy significantly affected both user effort and prediction accuracy. Popular items get many ratings but each carries little information ("if everyone likes Titanic…"). With random or high-entropy items, users may have to page through many screens before finding items they can rate; some simulated users had to view several hundred movies — [GroupLens PDF](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/voi-final.pdf); [ACM DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1145/604045.604052)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** In Rashid et al. the simulated signup asked for about 30 ratings, and McNee et al. (2003, UM, "Interfaces for eliciting new user preferences") compared system-chosen vs user-chosen items in MovieLens signup. User-chosen items took longer and gave somewhat lower accuracy but higher user satisfaction and sense of ownership. That point is relevant because a favourites list is fully *user-chosen*.
- Cremonesi et al. (polimi) frame the core design choice as how many ratings to collect before recommending: does accuracy keep increasing with profile length, and does the extra burden lower perceived quality? (The full paper returned 403, so the curve was not read) — [polimi PDF](https://re.public.polimi.it/bitstream/11311/692316/3/p27-cremonesi.pdf)
- Recent elicitation work (Greedy SLIM, decision-tree elicitation) still treats choosing a small, highly informative item set as the main lever for cold start, and notes accuracy suffers for users with few interactions — [arXiv 2406.06061](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.06061); [arXiv 2510.27342](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.27342)
- Choosing items from a list takes less effort than rating items — [CEUR Vol-1679](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1679/paper4.pdf)
- MUSIC model (Rentfrow, Goldberg & Levitin 2011, *JPSP*): five music-preference factors were derived from reactions to unfamiliar excerpts (about 52 songs per a Scientific American summary; a 2023 replication cites 94 excerpts in the original pool). The two figures may refer to different stimulus sets. The 2023 *Musicae Scientiae* replication reproduced the Sophisticated and Intense factors almost exactly but only partly reproduced the rest — [SAGE 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649231167488); [Scientific American blog](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/guest-blog/anything-but-country-what-factor-analysis-reveals-about-our-tastes-for-tunes/)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Rentfrow & Gosling (2003) built their 4-dimension genre model (Reflective & Complex, Intense & Rebellious, Upbeat & Conventional, Energetic & Rhythmic) from the STOMP, a 14-genre rating questionnaire. As I recall, its 3-week test-retest reliability was high (roughly .77–.89 at dimension level). This suggests *genre-level* taste is stable over weeks even from about 14 ratings. Dimension names confirmed — [zhenzhi record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/907772817)

### Inferences
- A 10–50 item favourites list is in the same range as classic signup elicitation (about 10–30 items), but it is *user-chosen and positive-only*. In practice it is more like McNee's user-chosen condition: more satisfying and more identity-laden, but less informative per item when users pick very popular items.
- Profile stability is likely much higher at the *dimension/genre* level than at the item level. Computing similarity on aggregated features (genres, MUSIC-like dimensions, audio or text embeddings) should stay stable with about 10 items per domain. Item-overlap similarity (exact same songs) will be noisy and sparse.
- Popular items carry little discriminative information (Rashid). Similarity should down-weight very popular favourites, for example with IDF-style weighting.

### Gaps
- No verified "accuracy plateaus at N ratings" figure was retrieved.
- No test-retest study of favourites lists (e.g. "top 10 films" re-elicited weeks later) was found.

## 4. Explicit favourites vs implicit feedback (plays) in recommender literature

### Takeaway
On Last.fm, explicit "loved" tracks and implicit play counts perform similarly as recommendation inputs and complement each other. Explicit signals are scarcer but considered more accurate about preference. Favourites lists are one-class (positive-only) data, which pushes the choice of similarity measure towards set-overlap or embedding methods rather than rating correlation.

### Cited Findings
- Jawaheer, Szomszor & Kostkova (2010, HetRec @ RecSys), Last.fm: implicit (play counts) and explicit positive feedback (loved tracks) complement each other and give similar recommendation performance despite their different characteristics. Explicit feedback is scarce while implicit is abundant. Explicit feedback is described as generally more accurate at representing preference — [UCL discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10088997/7/Jawaheer%2C%20Szomszor%2C%20Kostkova%20-%202010%20-%20Characterisation%20of%20explicit%20feedback%20in%20an%20online%20music%20recommendation%20service%281%29bh.pdf); [HetRec version](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10088957/1/Jawaheer%20-%20hetrec2010_paper_07%281%29bh.pdf)
- A study cited in a patent reports that 78.3% of 21,688 Last.fm users (16,973) used the "love" function. Most users will give explicit favourites when the function exists — [USPTO patent 9189802](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9189802) (secondary citation)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Hu, Koren & Volinsky (2008, ICDM, "Collaborative filtering for implicit feedback datasets") and Pan et al. (2008, ICDM, "One-class collaborative filtering"): with positive-only data, missing does not mean disliked. Methods must treat unobserved items as weak negatives or unknowns. Pearson-style rating correlation is undefined; Jaccard/cosine on binary sets or latent-factor embeddings are standard.
- No source found that directly compares explicit favourites vs play histories for predicting *user-user similarity or homophily* (as opposed to recommendation accuracy).

### Inferences
- With only small curated lists, exact item overlap between two users will often be zero (sparsity). Similarity should go through shared item features or embeddings (artist, genre, director, mood, MUSIC-like dimensions, text) rather than raw intersection.
- The "loved" signal is retrospective and identity-laden (section 1). This suits matching people and is weaker for predicting what they will consume next.

### Gaps
- No direct empirical comparison found of "loved" vs "played" for predicting friendship or similarity.

## 5. Lists as structured self-expression (playlist curation, ordering, list context)

### Takeaway
Personal playlists are a form of collecting and self-expression that gives a sense of ownership (Hagen 2015). Their meaning depends on purpose: thematic coherence, transitions and context. A "my favourites" list and a "party" list therefore measure different constructs.

### Cited Findings
- Hagen (2015), "The Playlist Experience: Personal Playlists in Music Streaming Services", *Popular Music and Society* 38(5):625–645. A mixed-methods study of how streaming users create, curate, maintain and use playlists. User control drives distinct playlist practices that are new forms of collecting but also continue pre-digital collecting — [UiO full text](https://duo.uio.no/bitstream/10852/58625/1/Playlist-experience-ANHagen-2015.pdf)
- Hagen in NPR: "I assume responsibility for what I'm listening to through the playlists because I created them" (ownership and identity) — [NPR 2015](https://www.npr.org/sections/therecord/2015/06/04/411795140/looking-for-a-connection-in-an-infinite-jukebox)
- Later work citing Hagen notes that playlist creation depends on track transitions and on musical or thematic coherence — [UMUAI 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11257-019-09237-4); [ISMIR 2016](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2016/paper/000087.pdf)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Cunningham, Bainbridge & Falconer (2006, ISMIR, "More of an art than a science"), and Spotify/academic analyses of playlist titles (e.g. Pichl et al.): playlists are commonly organised by *context/activity* (workout, party, sleep) or mood rather than "favourites". Context-labelled playlists predict situational use, not core taste.
- No source found that measures the information content of *rank order* within a top-N favourites list for similarity.

### Inferences
- Treat list context as a first-class field. A "favourites / defines me" list should feed the identity/taste profile. A "for parties" list should feed event or vibe matching.
- Rank order (e.g. the #1 favourite) probably carries extra weight, but there is no evidence for this; simple rank-decay weighting is a reasonable heuristic to test.

### Gaps
- No empirical work found on top-N rank information for similarity, or on how list length changes what people include.

## 6. Does a shared explicit favourite predict liking or attraction more than shared consumption?

### Takeaway
Shared displayed music and film tastes predict friendship formation in real data (Lewis et al. 2012). In experiments, shared music taste increases attraction mainly because people infer shared *values* from it (Boer et al. 2011). I found no study comparing shared favourites with shared consumption history head to head.

### Cited Findings
- Boer, Fischer, Strack, Bond, Lo & Lam (2011), "How Shared Preferences in Music Create Bonds Between People: Values as the Missing Link", *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*. Participants liked targets whose music taste matched their own, and this effect was mediated by perceived value similarity. A follow-up ruled out perceived personality similarity as an alternative explanation. In a roommate field study, similar music preferences correlated with similar values — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/why-music-moves-us/201108/music-and-the-ties-that-bind); [VUW record](https://wgtn.ac.nz/cacr/research/values/sociocultural-differences-in-self-construal-and-subjective-well-being); [in-mind](https://www.in-mind.org/node/385)
- Lewis et al. (2012, PNAS): shared *listed* favourite music and movies (not books) predicted later Facebook friendship among about 1,400 students over 4 years. Selection dominated over influence — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/01/taste-test/)
- Rentfrow & Gosling (2006) Study 1: music is the most common topic when strangers get acquainted. Taste disclosure is a natural opener — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507064/)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Bowman's Facebook favourites study named in the brief: I could not identify or verify it.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Byrne's similarity-attraction paradigm (1971) and Montoya et al.'s meta-analysis (2008, *JSPR*): *perceived* similarity predicts attraction more strongly than *actual* similarity, especially in real interactions. This supports showing a visible "% similar" based on displayed lists.

### Inferences
- Because attraction runs through *perceived* similarity and *inferred values*, a visible match based on displayed favourites may drive liking more than a hidden behavioural-similarity score would. The displayed overlap is also what users can perceive and talk about.
- Lewis's finding that books did not predict friendship suggests domain weights should not be equal. Music and film may be stronger social-matching domains than books (from one cohort; replication unknown).

### Gaps
- No head-to-head comparison found of shared favourites vs shared consumption logs for predicting liking.
- Boer et al. effect sizes and N not retrieved.

## 7. Practical implications: prompt and framing for creating lists

### Takeaway
I found no direct experimental evidence on prompt wording for favourites lists ("10 songs that define you" vs "what you listen to"). The evidence above implies the framing chooses which construct you measure: identity/aspirational taste or actual consumption.

### Cited Findings
- Future-oriented and public choices shift towards "should" or identity items (Milkman et al. 2009; Liu 2007), so prompts emphasising identity or display will bias lists towards prestige and differentiation — [UPenn](https://repository.upenn.edu/oid_papers/161); [synapsesocial](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2070119988)
- User-chosen elicitation feels better and gives more sense of ownership; choosing from a list costs less effort than rating — [CEUR Vol-1679](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1679/paper4.pdf); McNee 2003 **[UNVERIFIED]**
- Low-information popular items dominate when users choose freely — [Rashid et al. 2002](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/voi-final.pdf)

### Inferences (design suggestions, not tested)
- Use two prompts per domain or let the user pick a list type. "Defines me / all-time favourites" feeds identity matching. "On repeat lately / what you actually play" captures actual taste and guilty pleasures; it could be private, which may reduce self-presentation pressure.
- Ask for about 10 items per domain as a floor, which matches the top-10 paradigm where strangers already infer personality. Show similarity at genre/dimension level so sparse item overlap does not make scores look like zero.
- Optionally nudge towards distinctive picks ("pick some you think few people know") to counter low-information popular choices. This does risk amplifying differentiation-type performance.
- Let users mark a #1 or ordering, and record list context (favourites vs party) as metadata.

### Gaps
- No study found on prompt-wording effects for favourites elicitation. This is a clear candidate for an in-app A/B test, e.g. measuring stability, the correlation of displayed similarity with later reported liking at events, and how many items users enter.
